# Day 4 — First Session: International Commercial Terms / Incoterms (by Ms. Manisha Thaker, 22.07.21)

## Overview
This session, led by guest speaker Ms. Manisha Thaker, explains **Incoterms (International Commercial Terms)** — the standardized global rules that define who (buyer or seller) is responsible for transportation, cost, risk, and customs clearance at each stage of an international shipment. It covers the four Incoterm groups, key terms like FOB and CIF, and closes with a Q&A on dry ports, packaging, documentation, and choosing the right logistics partner.

## What Incoterms Are and Who Regulates Them
- Incoterms are a set of internationally standardized trade terms published and regulated by the **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC)**, headquartered in **Paris, France**. India is a member country and Indian exporters must follow ICC's Incoterm rules.
- Incoterms are **universally recognized** — accepted by courts, government authorities, banks, and financial institutions worldwide, removing ambiguity between traders from different countries, languages, and legal systems.
- Incoterms are periodically revised (referenced version discussed was **Incoterms 2020**, the latest at the time).

## What Incoterms Define — and What They Don't
Incoterms only define:
- **Delivery obligations** — at what point the seller has "delivered" the goods.
- **Transfer of risk** — the exact point where risk of loss/damage passes from seller to buyer.
- **Cost allocation** — which party pays for transportation, loading/unloading, insurance, and customs clearance at each stage.

Incoterms do **NOT** define:
- **Quality or quantity of goods** — that is agreed separately between buyer and seller.
- **Payment terms** — payment methods like **Letter of Credit (LC)** or **documentary collection** are negotiated separately and are not part of the Incoterm itself.
- Incoterms are only part of the overall sales contract, not the entire contract.

## The Four Incoterm Groups
1. **Group E (Ex Works / EXW)** — Minimum responsibility for the seller. The seller simply makes goods available at their own factory/premises; the buyer arranges all transportation, export clearance, and takes on virtually all risk and cost from that point.
2. **Group F (e.g., FCA, FAS, FOB)** — The seller delivers goods to a carrier nominated by the buyer (e.g., loads onto the ship/truck) and clears export customs; risk transfers once goods are handed to the carrier. The buyer arranges and pays for the main international carriage.
3. **Group C (e.g., CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP)** — The seller arranges and pays for the main carriage (and insurance, in the case of CIF/CIP), but risk still transfers to the buyer at the loading point (e.g., once goods are on board the ship) — so the seller pays freight but the buyer bears the risk during transit.
4. **Group D (e.g., DAP, DPU, DDP)** — Maximum responsibility for the seller. The seller delivers the goods all the way to the buyer's destination/door, in the case of **DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)**, even covering import duties and customs clearance in the destination country — the buyer simply waits to receive the goods.

## FOB and CIF — Key Terms Explained
- **FOB (Free On Board)**: Always quoted with a **named port of loading** (e.g., "FOB Chennai," "FOB Mumbai," "FOB Hyderabad" as illustrative examples) — cost and risk transfer to the buyer once goods are loaded onto the ship at that port.
- **CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight)**: the quoted invoice price includes the product cost, insurance, and freight to the destination port — the seller arranges and pays for insurance and shipping, but risk still passes to the buyer once goods are loaded at the origin port.
- Insurance cost under CIF is typically calculated as a percentage over the invoice value (to also cover potential profit margin loss), not just the bare goods value.

## Choosing an Incoterm — Practical Guidance
- Less experienced exporters often prefer terms with **smaller scope of responsibility** (e.g., FOB or EXW) since they involve less logistics coordination.
- More experienced exporters can offer **CIF or DDP** to be more competitive/convenient for the buyer — taking on more responsibility (and cost) but often commanding a better price/margin as a result, since buyers value a "door-to-door" experience.
- Before quoting any price, exporters should calculate the full **landed cost** in a spreadsheet: product cost + desired margin + transportation cost + customs clearance charges + import duties — not just the base product price.
- Always maintain good relationships with **reliable transport/logistics partners** and request proper quotations; don't select a logistics partner on price alone — track record and service reliability matter, since damaged, delayed, or lost shipments become the exporter's problem to resolve with the buyer.

## Transportation Modes
- Shipments are often **multimodal** — e.g., by road from factory to port, then by sea for the international leg, and again by road/rail at the destination.
- **Sea transport** is the most widely used and economical mode for international trade (majority of world trade volume moves by sea); air transport is used for smaller, urgent, or high-value/perishable goods.

## Documentation & Customs
- Proper documentation is essential — this includes the **commercial invoice, packing list, and Bill of Lading** (the shipping document confirming goods were loaded/shipped).
- Customs clearance (export side in India, import side in the destination country) requires accurate documentation; incomplete or incorrect paperwork can delay or even damage the consignment (goods held up, demurrage charges, etc.).
- Packaging must meet **international packaging standards**, varying by product type (e.g., specific materials like thermocol/professional boxes for fragile goods).

## Q&A Highlights
- **Dry ports for landlocked exporters** (e.g., manufacturers near Delhi/Ludhiana far from a seaport): explained that **dry ports (Inland Container Depots)** allow exporters away from the coast to complete customs and container-loading formalities locally before goods are transported to the actual seaport for onward shipment.
- **FOB naming convention**: clarified that the port named in "FOB [City]" is the **port of loading**, not necessarily the exporter's home city — the exporter should specify accurately which port the goods will be loaded from.
- **Choosing insurance**: coverage and cost depend on the specific insurance company's policy and the type of goods being shipped (fragile/perishable goods may need specialized coverage).
- **Import inspection query** (from an importer side): general guidance that goods can be inspected through proper customs and import documentation channels regardless of which part of India they're headed to.

## Key Takeaways
- Incoterms exist solely to define **delivery point, cost split, and risk transfer** — not product quality, quantity, or payment terms.
- The four groups (**E, F, C, D**) represent an increasing scale of seller responsibility, from Ex Works (minimum) to DDP (maximum).
- **FOB** and **CIF** are the most commonly referenced terms — always pair them with a specific named port.
- Calculate your **full landed cost** (product + margin + transport + duties + clearance charges) before quoting any Incoterm-based price.
- Proper **documentation** (invoice, packing list, Bill of Lading) and a **reliable logistics partner** are essential to avoid disputes and shipment damage.
