# Export Insurance — Plain-English Reference

## What Is Export Insurance?

When you ship goods to a buyer in another country, two big risks appear:

1. **The goods get damaged or lost on the way** (fire, sinking, theft, rough seas).
2. **The buyer doesn't pay you** (goes bankrupt, refuses the shipment, or their government blocks the payment).

Export insurance is a safety net that pays you money if either of those things happens, so one bad shipment doesn't wipe out your business.

There are two broad families:

| Family | Covers | Typical provider |
|---|---|---|
| **Marine cargo insurance** | Physical damage or loss of goods while they travel by sea, air, rail, or road | General insurance companies (e.g. New India Assurance, ICICI Lombard, HDFC Ergo) |
| **Export credit insurance** | Buyer not paying you (commercial risk) or a country blocking payment (political risk) | ECGC Ltd (a Government of India enterprise) |

You often need **both** — one protects the goods, the other protects the money.

## Marine Cargo Insurance

### How It Works

You (the exporter) or the buyer buys a policy before the shipment leaves. If the goods are damaged or lost during transit, the insurance company pays out.

### Who Buys It?

That depends on the **Incoterm** (a standard trade rule that says who is responsible for the goods at each stage):

- **FOB (Free On Board)** — your responsibility ends once the goods are loaded on the ship. The buyer usually buys the insurance.
- **CIF (Cost, Insurance & Freight)** — you include insurance in the price you quote. You buy and pay for the insurance.
- **CFR (Cost & Freight)** — you pay for shipping but the buyer covers insurance.

### Types of Cover

The Institute Cargo Clauses (ICC) set three standard levels:

| Clause | What it covers | Level |
|---|---|---|
| **ICC (A)** | All risks — everything except specific exclusions like war, strikes, delay | Broadest |
| **ICC (B)** | Named perils — fire, explosion, collision, earthquake, washing overboard, water damage | Medium |
| **ICC (C)** | Basic — fire, explosion, collision, general average, jettison | Narrowest |

Most exporters choose **ICC (A)** for full protection. War and strikes can be added for an extra premium (the price you pay for the insurance).

### Key Documents

- **Insurance certificate or policy** — proves the goods are insured. Banks need this for a Letter of Credit (LC).
- **Bill of lading (B/L)** — the shipping receipt that proves the goods were loaded. It also acts as a title document (whoever holds it can claim the goods).
- **Survey report** — if goods arrive damaged, a surveyor inspects them and writes this report. You need it to file a claim.

### How to File a Marine Cargo Claim

1. Notify the insurance company immediately when you discover damage or loss.
2. Get a survey done — the insurer or a third-party surveyor inspects the goods.
3. Gather documents: policy, bill of lading, invoice, packing list, survey report.
4. Submit the claim form and documents to the insurer within the time limit stated in the policy.
5. The insurer reviews and pays out if the claim is valid.

## ECGC — Export Credit Insurance

### What Is ECGC?

ECGC Ltd (formerly Export Credit Guarantee Corporation of India) is a Government of India enterprise that insures Indian exporters against the risk of not getting paid.

### What Risks Does ECGC Cover?

**Commercial risks:**
- Buyer goes bankrupt or insolvent.
- Buyer refuses to accept the goods (protracted default — a long, unexplained non-payment, typically 4 months overdue).
- Buyer simply doesn't pay within the agreed time.

**Political risks:**
- The buyer's government bans imports or cancels an import licence.
- War, revolution, or civil disturbance in the buyer's country.
- The buyer's country runs out of foreign exchange and cannot transfer payment.
- New laws in the buyer's country prevent payment.

### Main ECGC Policies for Exporters

1. **Standard Policy (Shipments — Comprehensive Risks)**
   - Covers both commercial and political risks.
   - Meant for exporters who ship regularly to multiple buyers and countries.
   - You declare all your shipments (whole-turnover basis) — you cannot cherry-pick only risky ones.

2. **Small Exporter Policy**
   - Simplified version for exporters with lower turnover.
   - Easier paperwork and lower premium rates.

3. **Specific Shipment Policy**
   - Covers one single shipment or contract.
   - Useful for a one-off large order.

4. **Buyer Exposure Policy**
   - Covers exposure to a specific buyer up to an approved limit.

### How ECGC Premium Works

- The premium (cost) is a small percentage of the invoice value.
- The exact rate depends on the buyer's country risk, payment terms, and your track record.
- Rates change periodically — always check the latest schedule on the ECGC website (ecgc.in) or ask their nearest branch.

### How to File an ECGC Claim

1. When payment is overdue, send reminders to the buyer and keep records of all communication.
2. If the buyer still doesn't pay after the **due date of payment + waiting period** (usually 4 months for commercial default), you can file a claim.
3. Submit: claim form, copy of the policy, invoice, shipping documents, correspondence with the buyer, and proof that you tried to recover the money.
4. ECGC investigates and, if the claim is valid, pays a percentage of the loss (the policy does not usually cover 100 % — the exporter bears a small share, typically 5–15 %).

## Common Questions

### Do I Need Insurance for Every Shipment?

It depends on your Incoterm and your risk appetite. If you sell CIF, you are contractually required to insure. For FOB, the buyer usually insures, but many exporters still buy their own policy for peace of mind. For ECGC, a whole-turnover policy covers all your shipments automatically once set up.

### How Much Does It Cost?

Marine cargo premiums typically range from 0.1 % to 2 % of the cargo value depending on the commodity, destination, packaging, and mode of transport. ECGC premiums are also a small fraction of invoice value. Exact rates change — contact the insurer or ECGC directly for the current schedule.

### What Is "General Average"?

If a ship is in danger and the captain has to throw some cargo overboard (or spend money) to save the whole ship, every cargo owner shares the cost proportionally. That sharing is called general average. Your marine insurance covers your share.

### What Is "Subrogation"?

After the insurer pays your claim, they step into your shoes and can try to recover the money from whoever caused the loss (the shipping line, the buyer, etc.). That right is called subrogation.

### What Are Incoterms?

Incoterms (International Commercial Terms) are standard three-letter codes published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) that define who pays for shipping, insurance, and customs, and at what point the risk transfers from seller to buyer. The most common for Indian exporters are FOB, CFR, CIF, and EXW (Ex Works — buyer handles everything from your factory gate).

### What Documents Do Banks Need for an LC Shipment?

For a Letter of Credit, the bank typically needs: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, insurance certificate (if CIF), certificate of origin, and any inspection certificates the LC specifies. Every document must match the LC terms exactly.

## Glossary

- **Premium** — the price you pay for insurance coverage, usually a percentage of cargo or invoice value.
- **ECGC** — Export Credit Guarantee Corporation of India Ltd, the government body that insures exporters against buyer non-payment.
- **Marine cargo insurance** — insurance that covers physical loss or damage to goods while they are being transported.
- **Bill of lading (B/L)** — the receipt from the shipping company proving your goods were loaded; also a title document.
- **Incoterms** — standard trade rules that say who pays for what (shipping, insurance, customs) and when risk passes from seller to buyer.
- **Letter of Credit (LC)** — a bank guarantee where the buyer's bank promises to pay you once you present the correct shipping documents.
- **Survey report** — an inspection report written after goods are found damaged; needed to file a marine insurance claim.
- **Whole-turnover policy** — an ECGC policy that covers all your export shipments, not just selected ones.
- **Protracted default** — when a buyer simply stops paying for a long time (typically 4+ months) without explanation.
- **General average** — a maritime rule where all cargo owners share the cost of saving a ship in danger.
- **Subrogation** — the insurer's right to recover money from the party that caused the loss, after paying your claim.
- **IRDAI** — Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, the body that licences and regulates insurance companies in India.
